A Pitch to Software Makers [September 14 2026]

Prologue

I rarely remember my dreams. I was tempted to start this essay by writing something about how the rising AI is haunting my sleep. Then felt it would have been deceitful. Maybe there is truth to AI haunting my sleep but I can’t really make a statement given that, most of the time, I have no recollection.

What I can say is that AI is haunting my day dreams. I watch the trajectory and reflect on past patterns and I feel a huge weight in my gut. I have the firm belief that this thing we’re being assaulted with is going to be a net negative and I’ve refused to take any active part in it. Today, we’re presented with sparkles and fireworks. Tomorrow, we’ll be left with social atrophy, a complete absence of meaning and destruction of our humanity.

Don’t leave just yet. I think it’s important to be open and blunt about the problems we have but not all hope is lost.

The Pitch to Software Makers

Here’s my pitch to software makers. And I’m not talking to enterprise software engineers that picked the industry as a safe and well-paying job. I understand if you had picked this as a career because of the benefits. You’re welcome to listen in but this is going to be me talking to the geeks that got into it for the pleasure of building. The joy of the craft.

Using LLMs / AI for your work should be offensive to you. It’s repulsive and it’s coming to steal what attracted you to this work in the first place. It might save you time on the things that appeared as friction (writing all those tests!) but that friction and the challenges were part of what made it more rewarding in the end.

Remember what it was like to start with an elegant idea, build it and watch it come to life. You built something. You paused and thought of a nice and clean architecture. Then you got to the APIs and did the same. You might have gotten a nagging feeling about some aspects of it but then another challenge surfaced and you realized that you could rewrite one part that would solve for that challenge and solve for that little nagging thing. Then you got to writing the code and get into a flow state. Writing, testing and iterating until things felt like they clicked. You lean back on your chair and think: it’s a beauty.

Of course, that’s not always as smooth as this. Deadlines and market pressures make you ship something before you feel like it’s ready. But you know it’s possible. That joyful experience of creation is what hooked you and kept you going.

One might say that the companies that are selling you LLMs are doing it for the pleasure of creation as well. The joyful experience of it might actually be relatable to you LLM makers. To this crowd, I say: it’s also fine for creations to be ephemeral. You had fun building Claude or ChatGPT. The challenges were interesting. It might even be beautiful, too. But it’s time to take a step back and realize this is growing to be a monster and the net result won’t be good. Kids are bailing out on learning because there’s no point and your neighbor is asking for life guidance from a chat bot that has no life experience. There’s no need to blame yourself. You were just doing it for the pleasure of building, too.

Now, we kind of know where this is going. We don’t know know it but we know if we’re being honest with our feelings. So, let’s just tear it all down. Let the tide roll in and wash it away. When the AI bubble comes crashing, tell your boss that it was good while it lasted and move on. Advocate against government bailouts. Tell your non tech friends that it’s a good thing and you’re happy that it’s over. If they tell you they loved chatting with Claude, offer them a listening hear and a real connection based on actual life experience.

Let’s try to bounce back by encouraging small creation. I’m not a politician or economist but if you must think in those terms, maybe this looks like investments in local businesses to get their chance at the joy of creation. Maybe try to help them. You might have some money in your savings and still have the craving for creation. It doesn’t even have to be with computers or software but it’s fine if it is. Yeah, it might be a though time and your leaders will say there is only one way. Don’t listen to them.

Listen to your intuition. The crash will be hell, sure. But we can all go outside, gather around, reconnect with one another and, to reuse Charles Eisenstein’s words, build a more beautiful world our hearts know is possible. And maybe we could finally find a way in that Hell to build a lasting Paradise.

Come on, it could be a lot of fun.

Epilogue: The words started flowing as I was in the shower this morning. Those are not the exact same words because I couldn’t be writing them as they came to me. The original words are gone but they had brought tears to my eyes. Ok fine, it could also have been water from the shower but the feelings were real.

I sometimes have people tell me I’m a pessimist because how vocal I am about the things I feel in my heart are wrong. I counter to them that accepting what you feel is wrong as a given is being pessimistic (and cynical, fatalistic). I’m vocal because I think there’s another way.

My wife is my toughest critic and she’ll read this and think my actions don’t match my big words and statements. She’ll say that I’m not such a big optimist all the time. Of course, she’s right. This is aspirational and it’s hard to be always authentic and true to your aspirations. I will admit that I don’t know exactly how we can build the Paradise from the upcoming AI bubble disaster but my gut tells me that this is the right way and right thing to try. If we don’t succeed, we’ll still know that the alternative would have been worse and the process will have been a rewarding adventure.
